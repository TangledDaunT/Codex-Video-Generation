# Storytelling and the motion stack

Applies to scripting, storyboards, generation prompts, and clip acceptance. These maintained editorial rules are self-contained and do not depend on a private attachment.

Treat retention techniques as creative hypotheses. The supplied guide's numerical attention-span, dopamine, conversion, and ideal-duration claims are not established facts. Do not repeat them as research findings or override an approved 40-second brief with the guide's suggested 45–90-second length.

## Script structure and review

Build a complete script for human review in one place:

1. **Hook:** identify the subject and an audience-specific reason to care immediately. Check subject, stakes, simple language, and clear meaning. The opening visual should support the line.
2. **Lock-in:** establish why the next explanation is worth watching through relevant proof, a credible transformation, an honest contrast, a named method with a useful reveal, or a real objection. Do not manufacture proof or make a bigger promise than the video delivers.
3. **Body blocks:** each block advances one useful idea and supports it with a concrete demonstration, evidence, example, or reason. Prefer a meaningful nonobvious insight to an obvious list. Keep sentence-to-sentence logic clear.
4. **Rehooks:** transition to the next useful question or unresolved condition. Use them where the idea changes or curiosity would otherwise stall, not at a universal attention-span interval. A rehook needs a genuine new payoff.
5. **Payoff:** visibly and verbally answer the opening promise. End cleanly. Include a concise topic-aligned CTA when it serves the brief; do not add an unapproved offer, invented lead magnet, or extra runtime to force one.

The guide's sentence counts are drafting suggestions, not quotas. A short reel may combine lock-in and its first explanation, or use one well-earned rehook. Do not force two body blocks, two rehooks, a summary, and a CTA into a duration that cannot support them. Cut repetition and filler before speeding up delivery. Read the script aloud or time a realistic conversational read, allowing breath, gestures, and demonstrations.

For each storyboard beat, record **new information → question/promise opened → where it is answered**. Close the loops by the end. Give small useful answers along the way rather than deferring everything to the last second. Before approval, check that the opening is understandable, the middle teaches something, each transition follows logically, and the payoff matches what was promised.

## Five retention patterns from the user

Select the pattern that fits the story; combine only when natural. Wording below is illustrative, not mandatory copy or evidence of a guaranteed result.

| Pattern | How to use it | Requirement for the payoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Result first, process next** | Show an actual result or clearly labeled proposed outcome, then unpack how it is made. Each step reveals another useful part of the process. | Demonstrate the causal steps; never present a mockup as a measured achieved result. |
| **A/B expectation, C reveal** | Establish two plausible options, then reveal a more useful third explanation or method. Example shape: “You might try A or B. The missing piece is C.” | C must be concrete, relevant, and supported. Do not create a false choice or dismiss valid alternatives just to sound surprising. |
| **Common belief, supported contradiction** | State a recognizable belief and explain the specific situation where it fails or needs qualification. | Supply a reason, example, or evidence. Do not label a belief “common” or false without a basis, or invent a contrarian claim for attention. |
| **Better-way rehook** | After establishing a working approach, introduce a meaningful improvement: “But here's a better way…” | Show what improves, how, and any tradeoff. Avoid unsupported universal “best” claims. |
| **Necessary-condition rehook** | Expose the missing prerequisite: “But this won't work unless…” | Name and demonstrate the actual condition promptly. It must be necessary to this process, not manufactured suspense. |

Keep the delivery conversational. A rehook should sound like a person moving the explanation forward, with a new visual or demonstration when helpful. Repeated “but” lines without new substance are filler. Do not force every pattern into every project.

## Motion stack, layer 1: style base

Before generating any image or video, define the shared style base in the production plan:

| Lock | Record explicitly | Preserve across scenes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Subject** | Approved character/product reference IDs, visible physical details, wardrobe/materials, proportions, recurring props and location | Identity, product geometry/markings, and approved appearance; a prompt's subject name alone is insufficient |
| **Lighting** | Key-light source and direction, softness, fill/contrast, practical lights, time of day, and intended exposure | Direction, shadow behavior, skin rendering, and room-light relationships |
| **Color look** | Palette, white balance, skin tones, saturation, contrast, and highlight/shadow treatment | One coherent grade; no unexplained shot-to-shot hue or exposure jumps |

Carry these descriptions/reference IDs into every scene prompt and compare each render with the approved references. Lens, framing, camera movement, and action may vary purposefully. Changes to time, location, or mood must be intentional in the approved storyboard and have their own explicit variation. Color correction can reconcile small differences; it cannot repair a different face, product, or lighting geometry.

## Motion stack, layer 2: motion playbook

Use a reusable project playbook with shot ID, exact camera wording, subject/action wording, start/end framing, direction, duration, and why the move helps this beat. Begin with the templates below, adapt them to the approved scene, then preserve the exact tested wording. These are starting templates, not model-specific guarantees or claims that every move has already been tested successfully in Flow.

| ID / structure | Example camera prompt wording | Best use / limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **M01 — slow push-in** | “One continuous shot. Camera makes a gentle, constant-speed physical push toward the presenter, starting in a medium shot and ending in a medium close-up. Keep the same camera height and screen direction; no orbit or abrupt zoom.” | Bring attention to a key reveal. Keep the planned face/product in frame and ensure the environment stays anchored. |
| **M02 — restrained orbit** | “One continuous shot. Camera travels on a small, slow arc from the subject's front-left toward the front, keeping a constant distance and eye-level height. The subject remains planted; background parallax follows the camera naturally.” | Reveal shape or a spatial relationship. Specify the actual arc and endpoints for the scene; avoid full circles, new unseen product details, or crossing the dialogue axis without intent. |
| **M03 — handheld drift** | “Eye-level handheld shot with very slight, controlled operator drift and natural settling. Maintain the medium framing and clear eyeline. No rhythmic shake, floating camera, sudden sway, or rapid reframing.” | An informal creator explanation. Use restrained amplitude; do not obscure the screen, captions, or lip-sync. |
| **M04 — locked demonstration** | “Camera stays on a fixed tripod throughout this shot. Keep the screen, desk, and presenter positions stable. Only the scripted subject action moves; preserve all background object positions.” | Clear instruction or a continuity anchor. Specify what the hands/person actually do so stable framing does not mean frozen action. |
| **M05 — planned editorial cut** | “This shot holds a stable [specified framing] while [specified action] reaches [specified end pose]. End on that pose for the next shot's matching start frame.” | Generate distinct compatible shots, then make a deliberate cut locally. Describe both sides of the cut in the storyboard; do not rely on unexplained generated camera jumps. |

For every scene, combine **style-base description + reference attachments + action beats + selected camera wording + exact dialogue/performance + end state + specific continuity constraints**. State duration using the actual supported UI setting. Keep the command physically plausible and focused; don't stack push-in, orbit, zoom, and handheld instructions together just to sound cinematic.

Subject motion and camera motion are separate. Describe hand contact, body weight, gaze shifts, object motion, and when each action occurs. For presenter scenes, preserve visible, synchronized speaking and the existing voice/performance checkpoint. Camera movement cannot replace useful teaching or expressive delivery.

## Motion stack, layer 3: quality gate

Review the full moving clip and audio, including opening/middle/ending, before keeping it. A good thumbnail is not a pass. Compare both within the shot and across its edit boundaries:

- **Anatomy and shape:** stable hands/fingers, face, body, object proportions, product geometry, and markings; reject warping, duplication, or changing identities.
- **Temporal surfaces:** stable clothing, skin, hair, background textures, lettering, light/shadow, and edges; reject flicker, crawling patterns, and unexplained exposure/color pulses.
- **Weight and contact:** believable acceleration, gravity, gestures, walking, hand/object contact, and camera parallax; reject floaty movement, foot/hand sliding, object penetration, and sudden unmotivated speed changes.
- **Style and continuity:** shared subject, lighting, and color look; fixed room layout and credible extras; no teleporting objects/people or inconsistent screen direction.
- **Performance and explanation:** correct visible speaker, natural eyeline, human rhythm, matching voice, lip-sync, intended action, and the information promised by the beat.

Record each scene's pass/fail, defect timestamp, likely correction, and affected neighbors in the manifest. Keep failures out of the accepted footage set. Repair a local edit/grade/caption issue without regeneration when that solves the actual defect. If generation is needed, revise only the failed scene's relevant prompt/reference/motion and any genuinely dependent boundary; reuse all passing clips. Respect existing approval and retry limits. Do not repeatedly spend credits on an unsupported shot or mask defects with aggressive blur.

Recheck the assembled reel for coherent style, natural motion, clear progression, and resolved curiosity promises. A visually clean reel still fails if it promises a result it never explains.
